### A note before you play

This is the story behind the man you're moving through the dark. Every mechanic in `index.html` — the cone of light a guard throws in front of himself, the way a whole floor turns on you the second one of them sees you, the way you flash red for a heartbeat when a bullet finds you, the way they duck behind a wall when you shoot back, the way the exit only opens once the room is quiet — all of it means something here. Play first, or read first. Either way, by the end, I hope the two versions of this start to feel like the same thing.

There's a short designer's note at the very end that connects the mechanics to the mythology directly, if you want the seams shown. I'd read the story first, though. Some things are better felt before they're explained.

## PROLOGUE — Four Minutes to the Chorus

The vault smells like cold metal and ozone, and Elias Marr is lying flat against a wall that hasn't been painted since before the Blackout, listening to a man's boots stop four feet from his skull.

He doesn't breathe. He has gotten very good, in three years, at not breathing.

The boots belong to a Sentinel — helmet-lamp swinging in a slow, bored arc, the beam sliding across the concrete a hand's width from his boot. If it touches him, every alarm in this building will know his name before he can stand up. That's the thing nobody tells you about the Lumen Grid until you've lived inside it: it was never really about seeing. It was about being seen, and never being allowed to forget it.

*Four minutes*, Echo had said, voice tight through the earpiece, *four minutes and the vault reseals with everyone in it who isn't tagged Concord. Get her out or get out. Don't you dare try both and get neither.*

Somewhere on the other side of that reinforced door is Naila. He has spent a year believing three different, contradictory things about whether that sentence could ever be true again, and he still isn't sure, lying here with his heart going too loud against the floor, which of those beliefs he's actually carrying into the room.

The Sentinel's radio crackles. *"Sweep clear, west corridor."*

The boots move on. The light slides away.

Elias lets out a breath a year overdue, and thinks: *rewind.*

*Let me tell you how a simple man ends up in the dark, not sure anymore what he's hoping to find on the other side of it.*

## Dramatis Personae

**Elias Marr** — Once a maintenance technician for the city's Lumen Grid, the network of Concord-mandated streetlights and checkpoint lamps that keep Halvern lit and watched around the clock. A quiet man. Good with his hands, better with silence, terrible at asking for help. Everything before was ordinary, and he liked it that way. Codename, eventually: **Moth** — because moths fly toward light out of instinct, and the whole of his training is learning not to.

**Naila Rey** — Archivist at the Halvern Municipal Records Office. Elias's fiancée. Quietly, carefully, for two years before anyone else knew it, something else entirely.

**Sable** — Goes by nothing else, not anymore. Runs the resistance cell known as the Undertow out of the drowned lower blocks of the city. Cold in a way that reads as competence until you've known her long enough to recognize it as grief wearing armor. Before the Undertow, she designed behavioral doctrine for Concord's Sentinel corps — she taught them how to hunt. She has spent every year since teaching people how not to be caught.

**Denny "Wire" Okafor** — Comms and systems. Twenty-six years old and allergic to silence, fills every dead radio channel with bad jokes and worse singing. Has a little sister still living under Concord registration on the surface, which is a detail that matters more than it seems like it should, later.

**Priya "Echo" Anand** — Former Sentinel field-training officer. Defected four years ago and has never once explained why, though everyone has a theory. Knows exactly how a Sentinel thinks, moves, ducks, and dies, because she is the one who wrote the manual. Steadier than anyone else in the Undertow, in a way that starts, over one long grieving year, to feel less like a professional trait and more like a place Elias keeps finding himself standing.

**Tomas "Latch" Rurik** — Explosives, locks, doors, and — as far as Elias is concerned — the closest thing to an older brother he has left. Slow to trust, impossible to shake once he does.

**Captain Hale Draeger** — Commands the Sentinel corps assigned to Vault security across the eastern district. Methodical, patient, and — this is the part that will cost Elias sleep — not cruel. Just utterly, unshakeably certain that the Lumen Grid is the only thing standing between Halvern and the dark that came before it. He has read Naila's intake file more times than duty required. He is the only person in Halvern who knows exactly what he did to her disposition log, and why.

**The Concord** — Not a man, not really a government anymore either. The administrative and military authority that took control of Halvern in the chaotic weeks after the Blackout, and never quite finished "temporarily" running the city. Everyone under fifty remembers a version of life before it. Everyone under twenty doesn't.

## A Primer on Halvern

Nine years ago, the city of Halvern went dark for eleven days. No power, no comms, no traffic grid, no hospital backup past the first forty hours. People still argue about what caused the Blackout — a grid failure, sabotage, an act of God, depending which corner you ask in. What nobody argues about is who fixed it.

The Concord arrived on day six with generators, floodlights, and enough armed personnel to make the floodlights feel less like a gift and more like a down payment. By day eleven, Halvern had light again, and a new government it had never voted for, and a promise: *never again.*

The promise took physical shape as the **Lumen Grid** — a city-wide network of Concord-controlled lighting, checkpoints, and surveillance lamps that never fully switch off. Streets that go dark are called **dead zones**, and dead zones are treated as an emergency, because a dead zone is a place the Concord temporarily can't see, and a place the Concord can't see is — officially, in the language of every curfew notice ever printed — "an active threat vector."

The soldiers who patrol the grid's more sensitive sites are called **Sentinels**. Every Sentinel carries a helmet-lamp tuned to the Lumen frequency, throwing a narrow cone of light out ahead of them that isn't just illumination — it's a live feed. Step into that cone, and in the same half-second the Sentinel sees you, so does every other Sentinel on the local relay. Halvern has a name for that shared, instant, building-wide alarm: **the Chorus**. Nobody who has heard it start ever forgets the sound.

Secure Concord facilities — records archives, weapons stores, anything the Concord doesn't want the public asking about — are called **Vaults**. Every Vault has an exit that stays sealed for as long as a single Sentinel inside remains standing. It's meant to be a security feature. The Undertow calls it something else: a promise that if you're patient and quiet and willing to take everyone in the room off the board, the door will, eventually, open for you too.

Beneath the Concord's floodlit streets is the **Undertow** — old flood-control tunnels and drowned subway levels that the Concord never bothered fully mapping, because who builds a resistance in a basement that floods twice a year? Sable did. That's most of the point of her.

## ACT I — The Ordinary Light

Before any of it, Elias Marr's life had a shape so small and warm you could hold the whole of it in two hands.

He fixed lamps. Not glamorous work — he'd climb the checkpoint poles at the edge of Sector 9 with a tool belt and a thermos of bad coffee and coax dying Lumen units back to life, one flickering bulb at a time, while beneath him the city went about its lit, watched, orderly business. He liked the work more than he ever admitted to anyone but Naila. There was something honest about it. A light works or it doesn't. You fix it or you don't. Nothing about a burnt-out bulb had ever lied to him.

Naila lied to him for two years, and it would be the truest thing anyone ever did for him.

They met the way small, ordinary things meet — a records request he'd filed to trace an old maintenance blueprint, a woman behind the archive counter who told him, deadpan, that his blueprint request was the seventh-most boring thing she'd read that week, and did he want to know what the other six were. He did. He always would, after that.

She had a laugh that arrived a half-second before she meant it to, like it kept escaping ahead of her. She kept a paper notebook, real paper, which was itself a small quiet act of stubbornness in a city that had digitized everything worth remembering and, not coincidentally, everything worth controlling. She used to fall asleep mid-sentence explaining some historical footnote from the pre-Blackout archive nobody else had asked to see in a decade, and Elias used to lie there and listen to her breathing change and think, with an ordinary, un-dramatic certainty: *this. Whatever this is, I want the whole rest of my life to be made of it.*

He proposed on the observation deck above Sector 9, badly, nervously, with a ring he'd been paying off for four months on a maintenance technician's salary, and she said yes before he finished the sentence, which became a joke between them for the rest of that year — *you never even let me ask.*

He didn't know, then, that she'd spent that same year quietly copying pre-Blackout grid schematics into a notebook that never went into any Concord database. He didn't know the "boring" records requests she filed on slow afternoons were tracing something specific: the Concord's own maintenance logs from the eleven days of the Blackout, cross-referenced against emergency generator deployment records that had been sitting, fully staged, in three Concord depots four days *before* the grid went down.

He found out on a Tuesday. He remembers it was a Tuesday because he'd fixed eleven lamps that day, a personal record, and he was going to tell her about it over dinner like it mattered.

Four Compliance Division vehicles were already parked outside their apartment block when he turned the corner. No sirens. The Concord had learned a long time ago that sirens gave people time to think.

He ran. He will spend the rest of his life not entirely forgiving himself for how fast, and how uselessly.

They had her on the steps already — two Sentinels, plainclothes Compliance officer between them holding a data slate up like a verdict already rendered. Naila wasn't struggling. That was the detail that broke something in him before anything else did: she wasn't struggling, because she had clearly already decided, in whatever silent arithmetic she'd been running for two years, that this was a cost she'd agreed to pay before he ever knew there was a ledger.

"Elias—" she started, and that was the only word of hers he'd get to keep for a long time after.

He got close enough to grab a Sentinel's arm before the other one's baton found the side of his skull, and the world did the thing it does in every account he's ever heard of a beating — it went white, then wrong, then very far away. He remembers the ground. He remembers, with a clarity that will outlive everything else from that night, Naila's voice pitched high and furious and *aimed*, not at him, at them — *if you kill him I will never give you one more word, do you understand me, not one* — and then nothing, for a while that the Concord's own incident log would later record, with bureaucratic indifference, as "subject rendered non-viable, remanded to dead zone disposal, Sector 9 perimeter."

*Dead zone disposal.* They didn't even bury people who didn't matter. They just put them somewhere the lights didn't reach, and let the dark finish the job the baton had started.

The dark did not finish the job.

## ACT II — What the Dark Teaches

He woke to a lantern, not a Lumen lamp — warm, yellow, unmistakably not the city's — and a woman's face he didn't know, studying him with the specific patience of someone who has watched a lot of people wake up broken and has stopped being surprised by it.

"You're going to want to not sit up yet," Sable said. "You've got a skull fracture that disagrees with gravity."

He sat up anyway, because some things you learn about a man on the worst night of his life, and stubbornness was apparently one of the true ones about Elias Marr. She let him have the two seconds it took to regret it.

"Where—"

"Underneath everything you used to think was the whole city," she said. "We call it the Undertow. You're welcome for the rescue, by the way. Wire spotted your heat signature on a dead-zone sweep. We don't usually pull civilians out. You should feel special."

"My—" His throat didn't want to make the word. "They took her. Naila. My—"

"I know who they took." Something moved behind Sable's expression, there and gone — and then, rarer than he'd learn to expect from her, a hesitation. Sable did not usually hesitate before delivering hard information. "I need you to hear this once, clean, and I'm not going to make you ask twice." A breath, deliberately even. "Archive division flagged her a Class Two research irregularity six weeks before the raid. Compliance doesn't sit on irregularities that size. They close them, fast, before anyone starts asking why the file existed in the first place." She let the next word land exactly as hard as it needed to. "We pulled the disposition log this morning, once we had your name to cross-reference against hers. NR-114. Status: terminated, three days post-intake. I'm sorry. I wish I had a version of this that let you sit up and go do something useful with the anger instead of carrying the rest of it too."

He didn't say anything for a long time. There wasn't a version of language built for what the inside of him was doing.

"How sure," he finally managed, "are you."

"As sure as their own paperwork lets anyone be." Sable's voice had gone very quiet. "I spent twelve years learning exactly how honest that paperwork usually is. I have no reason tonight to think this page of it is any different."

She would remember, much later, that sentence as the single worst professional misjudgment of her life. She did not know that yet. Neither did he.

"Sit. Down," she said again, gentler this time, when he tried to stand. "Whoever you're going to become because of tonight — and you are going to become someone, I promise you that much — that man doesn't get built out of grief alone. I can teach you what he needs instead. I can't make you want to learn it. That part's yours."

He looked at his hands — still a lamp technician's hands, calloused from checkpoint poles — and found, underneath the wreckage of everything, one thing still standing steady enough to hold onto.

"Teach me."

---

Training, it turned out, was mostly about learning to be afraid of the right things, and about discovering how much of a person can keep functioning around a hole that never quite closes.

Echo ran the first sessions, in a flooded transit platform lit only by the crew's own hand lanterns, and she did not go easy on him.

"A Sentinel's helmet-lamp isn't a light," she told him, walking him slow circuits around a mocked-up patrol route while Latch heckled from the sidelines. "It's a leash with a bulb on the end. Wherever it's pointed, that's the only direction that Sentinel can see you — dead ahead, in a cone about this wide." She held her hands up, a wedge of space between them. "Get outside that wedge and you could be standing close enough to touch him. Get inside it, and it doesn't matter if you're a mile away in the dark past his lamp's real reach — the second the geometry lines up, he's got you."

"And if he does?"

Her face did something complicated. "Then you'd better already be moving, because in about a second and a half, every other Sentinel on that floor is going to know exactly where you are too. We call it the Chorus. I used to help write the protocol for it, back when I still believed in what it was protecting." A pause, the kind that told him not to push. "Once one of them sings, Elias, they *all* come running. Doesn't matter if they're on the other side of the building with a wall between you and them. That's not a bug. I designed it that way on purpose. Took me four years on the other side of it to understand what I'd actually built."

He learned quickly that she was easiest to be around at the exact hours when grief made him hardest to be around at all — that she never once flinched from the silences he fell into mid-sentence, never once filled them with something cheap just to make them shorter. She'd simply wait, patient in a way that had nothing to do with training doctrine, until he found his way back to the surface of himself, and then pick the lesson back up exactly where they'd left it, like the gap had never happened.

He didn't notice, for a long time, how much that particular kind of patience was starting to feel like something he looked forward to.

Latch taught him doors — how a lock actually surrenders versus how it looks like it does in the stories, how to read a hinge for the sound it's about to make before it makes it. Wire taught him the comms discipline that kept a whole crew alive on nothing but whispers and hand signals, and somewhere in the third week of that, over bad coffee that reminded Elias uncomfortably of the thermos he used to carry up checkpoint poles, Wire told him, unprompted, about a little sister named Adaeze still living topside under Concord registration, still going to a Concord-run school, still — as far as Wire knew — completely unaware her brother was anything but the missing-presumed-dead statistic Concord's records called him.

"You ever think about telling her?" Elias asked.

"Every single day," Wire said, and didn't elaborate, and Elias — new enough to grief himself to recognize its shape in someone else without needing the rest of the sentence — didn't ask again.

His first real mission — a supply Vault, low security, meant to be gentle — went almost exactly the way Echo had drilled him for two months. Almost. He froze in a Sentinel's peripheral wedge for one held breath too long, took a round through the shoulder for it, lit up red across the relay for four agonizing seconds while Latch swore a blue streak in his ear and Wire rerouted a checkpoint camera loop to buy them a window — and made it out. Shaking. Alive. Blooded, in every sense the crew's dark humor found for the word over drinks that night.

Echo was the one who found him afterward, sitting alone on the tunnel's edge, staring at the dark water instead of at the small crowd celebrating twenty feet behind him.

"You're supposed to be in there letting Latch buy you a very badly-deserved drink," she said, sitting down beside him without asking.

"I keep doing the math," he said. "How many more of these until it stops feeling like this."

"It doesn't stop." She said it plainly, no comfort dressed up as an answer. "You just get more places to put it." A pause. "For what it's worth — the first year, I put most of mine into hating a system I used to be proud of building. Turned out to be a pretty sturdy container. Might try it."

He almost laughed, which surprised him more than it should have. "Is that professional advice or personal?"

"Ask me again," she said, echoing, without either of them noticing yet, the exact shape of the thing Wire had once told him about Adaeze, "when I actually know the answer."

Sable found them like that, twenty minutes later, still sitting at the tunnel's edge, not talking about anything in particular anymore, just sitting, and something in her face when she saw it made her turn around and walk back the way she'd come without saying a word.

## ACT III — The Slow Warmth

Eight months bought him a codename that stuck, a body that had relearned what quiet felt like, and a grief that had — exactly as Echo promised — stopped shrinking and simply found more places to live inside him.

It also, without either of them ever quite deciding it out loud, bought him Echo.

There was no single moment he could have pointed to later and called the beginning. It was the accumulation of a hundred small ones — her waiting out his silences instead of filling them, the specific quality of quiet that settled over both of them after a bad job, the night she showed up at his cot with two cups of something that was technically coffee and sat with him until the shaking in his hands finally stopped and neither of them mentioned that it had ever started. It was the way she said his real name, not the codename, in the handful of moments a day nobody else was near enough to hear it, like she'd decided somewhere along the way that Elias Marr deserved to still exist somewhere other than a disposal log, even if only in her mouth, even if only in private.

"You know this isn't nothing," she told him once, plainly, the way she told him everything, on a night neither of them had planned for the conversation to happen. "I'm not going to pretend it is just because the timing's inconvenient for both of us."

"I know."

"I'm also not going to pretend I know what you'd have wanted, if the world hadn't taken the choice away from you a year ago." Her voice, for once, wasn't perfectly steady. "So I'm just going to say the true thing and let you do whatever you need to do with it. I care about you. Not as your instructor. Not as your handler. I don't need you to say it back tonight, or ever, if it's not true. I needed it to exist outside my own head for once."

He didn't say it back that night. He also didn't tell her it wasn't true, because it would have been a lie, and he had promised himself, somewhere in the wreckage of the last year, that he would never again let someone he cared about carry a lie for his sake without knowing the weight of it.

What they built after that was quiet and unhurried and entirely without ceremony — nothing either of them would have called, out loud, a beginning, because starting something new still felt, to both of them, like it required a kind of permission neither had been offered. But it was real. It sat with him differently than grief did. It gave him, for the first time since the steps outside his old apartment, a reason to imagine a version of the future that wasn't only built out of vengeance.

The work went on around it, the way work does. Naila's old notebook — recovered, months earlier, from a resistance sympathizer inside Records who'd smuggled it out of her apartment before Compliance finished their sweep — had become, without anyone quite planning it, the Undertow's roadmap. Sable's people spent that season cross-referencing every page of it against the Concord's Central Compliance Archive, building toward one enormous, dangerous conclusion: the evidence that would prove, beyond any Concord denial, that the Blackout had been staged, was sitting inside a single climate-controlled records vault Naila herself had traced two years ago and never gotten the chance to open.

The Sentinel corps guarding that archive answered to a commander named Hale Draeger.

"I know him," Echo said, when the name came up, in a voice gone carefully flat. "Knew him. We trained together, before. He's not what you're picturing. He's not cruel for the sake of it. That's what makes him dangerous — he genuinely believes every Sentinel he's ever put a bullet in earned it by threatening the only thing standing between this city and eleven more days of dark. You cannot shame a man like that into stopping. You can only out-think him, or you can only stop him."

Elias carried Naila's notebook into every briefing after that, not because Sable required it, but because finishing the work in those pages had become, somewhere in the last eight months, the closest thing he had left to a way of still loving her out loud. Echo never once asked him to put it away. She was, in her own careful fashion, still learning how to be the kind of person who could hold space for someone else's dead without needing them to stop grieving in order to make room for her, and she was, more often than either of them said aloud, better at it than she gave herself credit for.

## ACT IV — What the Archive Remembered

They ran three lesser Vaults that season, bleeding intelligence toward the archive, each one costing them something small — a wounded hand, a burned safehouse, a week Latch spent unable to walk without wincing from a graze he wore, afterward, like a punctuation mark he refused to let anyone feel sorry for.

It was on the fourth job — the archive itself, finally, after eight months of preparation — that the crew's shape cracked for good.

They'd planned it clean: in, copy everything Naila's notebook pointed to, out, nobody the wiser. Wire had the archive's internal cameras looped inside four minutes flat, which was, even for him, fast. Too fast, in the small, cold part of Elias's mind that had spent eight months learning to notice when something in a room didn't sit right.

They were forty seconds from the exit when the archive's real alarm — not the Chorus, something older, quieter, a direct line — went off exactly where their planned route crossed it, as if someone had known, in advance, precisely which second and precisely which corridor to seed with a response squad.

They got out, but not clean. Latch took the position nobody else could hold long enough at the fallback door, laid down enough covering fire to buy Elias and Echo the four seconds they needed to drag the emergency bulkhead down between themselves and the converging squad, his voice on comms going from steady to strained to, finally, terribly quiet, right as the bulkhead sealed.

"Latch." Elias's hands were already at the manual release before Echo's grip stopped him. "Latch, say something—"

The channel gave them nothing but the low hum of a connection still technically open, technically alive, saying nothing at all, for the rest of that night and every night after.

They found out about Wire two days later, the same way they found out about everything the Concord wanted found a certain way — through a recording clearly meant to be heard, a Compliance officer's calm, bureaucratic voice looped once through Wire's own dead channel: *"Asset cooperation logged. Family registration status: upgraded, effective immediate. Thank you for your service to the Concord, Mister Okafor."*

Adaeze. Of course it was Adaeze. They'd found her months ago, held her registration like a leash nobody topside would ever notice was a leash, and given Wire the oldest, cruelest choice the Concord had ever perfected: his sister's safety, or his crew's. He had bled them one small compromised job at a time, buying her a little more safety with a little more of himself, until the archive job finally came due in full — and then, once he understood exactly what that debt had cost, hadn't been able to live inside the man it made of him.

They found, tucked inside his personal effects, alongside a data chip he'd clearly smuggled out of the archive's system in the last clean minute he had left in him — a final act of guilt or love or both, nobody would ever be entirely sure which — a single unsent message queued to Adaeze's registration terminal: *I did something so you'd get to keep being a kid a little longer. I don't need you to ever know what. I just need you to keep being one. Love you more than the whole stupid lit-up city. — D.*

They played it once, together, in the tunnel, and let it be the only funeral he got.

It was Sable, three sleepless nights later, who finally pulled the data off Wire's chip and understood, staring at it in the lantern-light with an expression Elias had never seen on her before, that the worst night of the last year was not, after all, finished handing out its casualties.

Buried four folders deep in a sub-archive nobody outside senior Compliance was ever meant to open was a disposition correction, filed and immediately re-sealed eleven months ago, quietly overriding an earlier termination entry for an intake tag Sable had cross-referenced, once, a lifetime ago, and never thought to check again: **NR-114 — status corrected: reclassified, extended hold. Location: Vault 12, eastern district. Authorizing officer: H. Draeger.**

Sable read it four times before she trusted herself to say it out loud. Then she found Elias in the tunnel's far corner, where he'd taken, in the months since Latch, to sitting with Naila's notebook open on his knees without reading it, and she did not soften a single word of what she had to tell him, because she had promised herself, a long time ago, never to do that to him again.

"She's alive," Sable said. "She has been for almost the whole year. I gave you the wrong page eleven months ago and never once thought to check it twice, and I am never going to forgive myself for that, and none of that is going to matter half as much right now as the fact that we know where she is." A breath. "Vault 12. Draeger's own signature kept her off a kill order that should have gone through on schedule. I don't know why yet. I intend to find out."

Elias did not say anything for a long time. Echo, two feet away, went very still in a way that told him she had already, in the span of one sentence, understood everything this meant for both of their futures at once, and had decided, with the same steady discipline she brought to everything, to let him have the moment before she had to have her own.

"Elias." Her voice, when it came, was quiet, and careful, and entirely without the resentment she would have been forgiven for feeling. "Go get her."

"Echo—"

"I mean it." She held his eyes, and whatever was moving behind her own was too complicated for either of them to name yet, that night, in front of Sable, in the wreckage of two fresh graves they hadn't even finished grieving. "Whatever this year was — I'm not going to pretend it wasn't real, and I'm not going to let you pretend it wasn't either, later, when it's convenient. But I have spent four years learning exactly what it costs to leave someone in a Concord facility when there was still a version of the world where you could have gone and gotten them out. I'm not going to be the reason that cost lands on you too." Her jaw tightened, once. "Go get your wife, Moth. We'll figure out the rest of it after, if there's a rest of it to figure out."

## ACT V — Ghostlight

Vault 12, the night they went in, was exactly the nightmare Sable had promised on the walk over — every Sentinel's helmet-lamp burning wider and further than anything Echo's old drills had prepared him for, every one of them moving like they expected him, cover-shifting and countering with a fluency that told Elias, cold and clear, that Echo's own doctrine had been refined since her defection into something meaner. The instant the first Sentinel's wedge found him, the Chorus didn't just wake the floor — it woke the whole building at once, every guard on every level converging on his position with a speed that made his first mission's terror feel, in retrospect, gentle.

He fought through it anyway, Echo and Sable at his back, because there was no other direction left to go, and because somewhere on the other side of that floor was the one person this entire impossible year had, it turned out, never actually stopped being for.

He found Draeger waiting for him outside the deep-hold cellblock, alone, deliberately, helmet-lamp off, sidearm holstered — a man who had clearly decided, at some point in the long hours of preparing for this night, exactly how he wanted this conversation to happen.

"Marr." Draeger said the name — his real name, not Moth, not the disposal-file ghost the Concord's records swore was already dead — with a kind of weary respect Elias hadn't expected and didn't want. "You want to know why the correction. Why I buried a kill order with my own signature and kept a Class Two irregularity breathing eleven months past her expiration date." He didn't wait for the question. "Because I read her intake file, and I read what she'd traced, and I understood, the same night, that she was right about all of it, and that whoever signed that first kill order was doing it to protect a lie I had already spent my whole career enforcing. I couldn't undo that lie. I could still, quietly, refuse to let it kill one more person who'd only ever been guilty of proving it true." His eyes didn't waver. "I'm not going to pretend that makes me the hero of tonight. I'm telling you because I think you've earned the truth more than almost anyone alive, and because I don't believe either of us gets the luxury of pretending anymore."

"Then let her go. All the way. Tonight."

"That part was never entirely mine to give," Draeger said, and something in his voice made the hair on Elias's arms rise before the man even finished the sentence. "The correction I filed was quiet enough to survive a year of audits. It will not survive me stepping aside and letting you walk her out the front of my own Vault. The moment I do that, Central Compliance stops trusting anything with my signature on it, going back years — which means every quiet mercy I've ever bought someone this way gets re-opened and re-examined by people considerably less inclined toward mercy than I've apparently become." He stepped aside from the cellblock door anyway, slow, deliberate. "The floor's going into full lockdown the second Central flags my override. Every exit but the primary corridor seals in minutes. I would move, if I were you, and I would not waste time being grateful. I'm not doing this for gratitude."

Naila was exactly where the file said, and nothing like the person that file had reduced her to — thinner, harder-eyed, a fading Compliance restraint-mark still healing on one wrist, and entirely, unmistakably, ferociously herself the instant she saw him standing in that doorway.

"You absolute idiot," she said, voice breaking on every single word, already crossing the distance between them. "You beautiful, reckless, *idiot*, they told me you were—"

"I know what they told you." He held on like the last year hadn't happened, like it had happened completely, both at once, the only way grief and relief have ever managed to occupy the same body. "I know. I'm here. I'm here."

Sable's voice cut through comms before either of them could say anything more. *"Lockdown's triggered. Central caught the override. Primary corridor's the only way out and it's sealing in under three minutes — and it's not sealing empty. There's a full response wave inbound through it right now."*

They ran for it together, finally, after a year of running toward each other from opposite directions through the dark, and reached the last stretch of corridor to find exactly what Sable had warned them of: a wave of Sentinels pouring in from the far end, and behind them, at the corridor's midpoint, an old maintenance alcove Elias recognized with a jolt — a pre-Concord archive relay junction, the kind of system nobody but a records specialist would know how to actually operate, wired straight into the Vault's structural failsafes.

Naila saw it in the same instant he did, and he watched, with a dread that arrived a full second before understanding did, her whole posture change.

"I can purge the relay," she said, already moving toward it. "It'll dump every failsafe in this corridor into overload — seals the response wave out for good, buys everyone behind me all the time they need to reach the exit. It's archive architecture, Elias. Old-world. I know exactly how to read it. Nobody else here does."

"Then show me. We do it together and we both run."

"It's a manual purge." Her hands were already on the panel, moving with a certainty that told him she'd already run this arithmetic to its end before she said a single word of it out loud. "Someone has to hold the relay open the whole time it dumps, or it fails halfway and takes the corridor with it — everyone in it. That's not a two-person job, and it's not a job either of us gets to walk away from once it starts." She looked at him, and everything that had happened to her in the last year, everything she'd survived to still be standing in front of him at all, was in her face at once. "You already tried to trade your life for mine once tonight, the second we saw that response wave. I watched you do the math. I'm not going to let you finish it." Her voice cracked, once, and she let it. "Let me have this one, Elias. You got two years of me already saying yes to costs before you knew there was a ledger. Let this be the last page of it, and let it be mine to choose."

"Naila—"

"*Go.*" She was already keying the relay, the panel beginning to hum with a rising, dangerous light. "Tell Adaeze what her brother actually did for her, if you ever get the chance. Tell Sable the notebook was worth finishing. And Elias—" her eyes found his, steady, fierce, entirely her own, "—whatever this year made of you, whoever you needed to become to get to this door — don't you dare let it make you someone who can't put it down again once you're through it. Go *live*, you idiot. That was always the whole point of any of this."

Echo's hand closed on his arm — not pulling him away, he'd understand later, but holding him upright, because his legs had very nearly stopped being willing to carry him anywhere at all.

The relay's purge caught the first wave of Sentinels three seconds after Elias, Echo, and Sable cleared the sealing exit behind them, the corridor behind them going white, then dark, then permanently, unbreachably silent. The door ground shut on a room that had, at last, gone entirely quiet.

It did not open again that night, for anyone.

---

They came up into a night sky Elias hadn't stood under, unwatched, in longer than he could measure, and he did not, for a long time, remember how to do anything with it but stand there and shake.

Echo did not try to fill the silence. She simply stayed close enough that he'd know, whenever he was ready to notice it again, that he hadn't been left alone in it — the same steady patience she'd given him for a whole year before either of them had a name for what it was building toward, offered now for an entirely different, far heavier kind of grief, without either of them pretending the two griefs were the same size, or owed the same amount of room.

## EPILOGUE — What the Room Remembers

Draeger was quietly reassigned three weeks later to a district with no active Vaults and considerably less discretion than he'd once had — the Concord's own machinery extending the closest thing to a mercy it was capable of, to a man who'd let a door open when his orders said to keep it shut, and paid for it in everything but his life.

Sable never forgave herself for the eleven months. She said so, once, plainly, the way she said everything, and never brought it up again, though Elias noticed, after that night, that she read every disposition log the Undertow ever recovered twice before she let herself believe a single word of it.

Wire's message finally reached Adaeze six months later, through channels nobody discussed openly, along with the truth about her brother the Concord's file had never once intended her to have. What she chose to do with either of those things belonged, from that day forward, entirely to her.

The notebook — Naila's notebook, real paper, stubborn as the woman who'd kept it — became, in the year that followed, the spine of everything the Undertow eventually put in front of the whole of Halvern: the staged Blackout, the depots stocked four days early, all of it, finally, undeniably public, carried the last distance by people who had never met her and would never know her name, only her handwriting.

Elias still fixes things. It turns out that was never really about the lamps.

Some nights, down in the Undertow's flooded lower platform, there's a lantern that burns a warmer color than any Lumen bulb ever has, and if you know where to look, you'll find him sitting under it, Naila's notebook closed now, finished, resting on the table beside him instead of open on his knees. Echo sits with him some of those nights, not every one of them, and neither of them has hurried to give whatever is quietly, carefully rebuilding itself between them a name it isn't ready for yet. Grief, it turns out, does not empty a person out to make room for the next thing. It just teaches them, slowly, how much more room they actually had than they thought.

The city is still lit. The city is still watched. Somewhere out past the tunnel mouth, right now, another Vault is waking up to the news that its exit only opens once the room goes quiet.

*Rewind*, Elias thinks sometimes, watching the lantern gutter low and catch again. *Let me tell you how a simple man ends up in the dark, waiting for a light to pass him by.*

*He gets very, very good at it. He loses more than anyone should be asked to carry out the other side. And somehow, some nights, under a lantern that isn't the Concord's to switch off, he still finds enough left standing in him to call it a life.*

## Designer's Note — Where the Story Lives in the Code

A few of the connections, laid bare, if you want them:

- **The vision cone / "torch light"** each guard casts is the **Wedge** — the narrow slice of world a Sentinel's helmet-lamp can actually see. Stay outside it, and distance stops mattering.
- **Guards alerting the instant one of them spots you, no matter where they are** is the **Chorus** — Echo's old alarm doctrine, shared instantly across the whole floor the moment one Sentinel's Wedge finds you.
- **Enemies hearing gunfire and investigating the source** are Sentinels reacting to sound the same way the Chorus reacts to sight — a quieter, more localized version of the same networked paranoia.
- **Guards dodging your shots and ducking behind cover** are running Echo's own field-training reflexes back at you — the doctrine she once wrote to keep Sentinels alive, now standing between you and the person you came for.
- **Flashing red when you're hit** is the Lumen tracer dye — you're not just hurt, for a few seconds you're the brightest, most findable thing in the room.
- **The exit only unlocking once every guard is down** is, quite literally, what Vault 12 finally cost Naila to open by hand — you earn the door by clearing the room, one way or another.
- **The map you wander, and the guards who patrol and search it long after losing you** — that persistence, that slow, patient searching — is the same patience Sable spent a year teaching Elias to survive, and the same patience Draeger spent a year quietly protecting a secret against.

The mechanics were never just mechanics. They were always the shape of the story, waiting for someone to notice.

Go earn the door.
